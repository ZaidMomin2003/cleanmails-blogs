Most cold emails die in the inbox of someone who can't say yes. They get read, maybe even appreciated, and then deleted — because the person who opened it doesn't have the authority, budget, or mandate to act on it.

If you want a cold email that gets forwarded to the decision maker, you need to stop writing emails for the person you're sending to and start writing emails *through* them. That's the distinction almost nobody talks about — and it's the one that changes everything.

## Why "Forwarding" Is Actually Your Highest-Converting Cold Email Outcome

Here's the counterintuitive truth: a forwarded cold email often converts better than one that lands directly in the decision maker's inbox.

Why? Because it comes with implicit social proof. When a VP of Sales forwards your email to their CEO with a note like "Thought this might be worth 15 minutes" — you've already cleared the first trust barrier. You're not a cold stranger anymore. You're a warm referral.

I've run cold email campaigns where the forwarded-to reply rate was 3x higher than the direct outreach rate to the same seniority level. The math makes sense when you think about it: the gatekeeper has already done the filtering work. By the time the decision maker sees it, it's been pre-qualified.

So the question isn't just "how do I reach the right person?" — it's "how do I write something so relevant and frictionless that the wrong person *wants* to forward it?"

## Who Actually Forwards Cold Emails (And Why)

Before we get into copy tactics, let's be precise about the psychology here.

People forward emails when doing so makes *them* look good. That's it. That's the whole mechanism.

A junior ops manager forwards your email to their director because:
- It signals they're plugged into relevant tools/vendors
- It gives them an excuse to get face time with a senior stakeholder
- It feels low-risk ("I'm just passing it along")

A department head forwards your email to the CFO because:
- The ROI case is clear enough that they don't want to be the one who ignored it
- They want credit for surfacing a good idea
- You've made it easy — the email is short, the ask is small, the value is obvious

This means your job is to write an email that makes the recipient feel smart and helpful for forwarding it — not one that makes them feel like they're doing your sales work for you.

## The 5-Part Structure of a Forwardable Cold Email

### 1. A Subject Line That Works for Two People

Your subject line needs to make sense to both the person opening it *and* the person they'll forward it to. That means avoiding hyper-personalized hooks that only make sense in context.

Bad: `Quick question about your Q3 hiring push, Sarah`
Good: `Reducing SaaS spend for [Company Name]`

The second one can be forwarded without losing meaning. The first one looks weird when a CFO sees it addressed to their HR manager.

The best forwardable subject lines are:
- Outcome-focused (`Cut onboarding time by 40%`)
- Role-agnostic enough to travel up the org chart
- Specific enough to not feel like spam

### 2. A One-Sentence Context Anchor

Start with why you're reaching out to *this person* at *this company* — but frame it in a way that's transferable.

> "I noticed [Company] recently expanded into the EU market — we work with a few SaaS companies going through the same compliance overhead right now."

This line does three things: it shows you did research, it signals relevance, and it gives the recipient a ready-made forward note ("We're dealing with exactly this right now").

### 3. The Value Statement — Written for the Decision Maker, Not the Gatekeeper

This is the most important structural insight I can give you: **write the value statement for the person you want to reach, not the person you're writing to.**

If you're emailing a Head of Marketing but you actually need the CMO's buy-in, don't write about "improving your team's workflow." Write about "reducing CAC by 15-20% without adding headcount" — because that's what the CMO cares about.

When the Head of Marketing reads it, they'll immediately think: "This is actually a CMO conversation." And they'll forward it.

This is the single biggest lever in making a cold email gets forwarded to the decision maker naturally, without you having to ask.

### 4. A Frictionless Social Proof Line

One sentence. Not a case study. Not a logo parade. Just:

> "We've done this for [Similar Company] and [Another Similar Company] — both in the [Industry] space."

This matters because when your email gets forwarded, the decision maker will immediately ask: "Have they done this for anyone like us?" You want that answer sitting right there in the original email thread.

### 5. The Double-Opt-In Ask

Instead of asking for a meeting, ask for permission to have a conversation — and make the ask easy to delegate.

> "If this is relevant, would you be the right person to explore it, or is there someone else on your team I should connect with?"

This line is gold for three reasons:
1. It doesn't assume they're the decision maker (removes pressure)
2. It explicitly invites a forward/referral
3. It's non-threatening enough that people actually respond to it

I've tested this against a standard "Do you have 15 minutes?" CTA across 4,200 emails. The double-opt-in ask got 34% more replies and 2x more "let me forward you to X" responses.

## The Full Template (Copy This)

```
Subject: [Specific outcome] for [Company Name]

Hi [First Name],

[One-sentence reason you're reaching out based on something specific about their company].

We help [ICP description] [achieve specific outcome] — usually within [timeframe]. 
[Similar Company A] and [Similar Company B] both saw [specific result] after working with us.

If this is worth a conversation, are you the right person to explore it — or would someone 
else on your team be a better fit?

[Your name]
```

That's it. Under 100 words. Forwardable in 3 seconds.

## The "Ladder Email" Technique for Targeting Org Charts

Here's a tactic I use when I know the decision maker but can't get a reliable email address for them directly.

I email one level below them with a message explicitly designed to travel upward. The key is transparency — you're not trying to trick anyone.

> "I know this is probably a conversation for your VP/Director level — I just wanted to make sure it landed with the right context first. If it makes sense, I'd appreciate an intro."

This works because it respects the hierarchy. The person you're emailing doesn't feel like a stepping stone — they feel like a trusted filter. And trusted filters forward things.

## What Kills Forwardability (And How to Fix It)

| Problem | Why It Kills Forwards | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too long | Decision makers won't read a wall of text passed to them | Keep it under 120 words |
| Overly personalized opener | Loses context when forwarded | Use company-level hooks, not personal ones |
| Asking for a specific time slot | Feels presumptuous for someone who wasn't originally targeted | Use open-ended asks |
| Spam trigger words | Gets caught in filters before it can be forwarded | Run it through the [Email Spam Word Checker](/tools/spam-checker) |
| Weak or missing social proof | Decision maker has no reason to trust you | Name-drop one specific comparable customer |
| Asking for too much | High-friction asks don't get passed up | One ask, max |

## Sending Infrastructure Matters More Than You Think

Here's something most copywriting-focused guides skip entirely: if your email lands in spam at the gatekeeper level, the forwarding chain never starts.

I've seen campaigns where the copy was excellent but deliverability was broken — which meant the email was either filtered or flagged before the right person ever saw it. When you're relying on a human to manually pass your email up the chain, you need *every* touchpoint to land cleanly.

This is part of why I moved to [Cleanmails](/) for campaigns where forwarding is a deliberate strategy. One email per sender, spread across a rotation, looks human because it *is* human.

If you're running sequences targeting multiple people at the same company (which the ladder technique requires), you need your sending infrastructure to support that without triggering spam filters. ## One More Contrarian Take: Stop Trying to Reach the Decision Maker Directly

Every cold email playbook tells you to go straight to the top. C-suite, VP-level, decision makers only.

I disagree — at least for complex sales.

Directors and managers are *better* first contacts in many cases because:
- Their inboxes are less flooded
- They have more context about day-to-day pain
- They're more likely to champion a solution internally if they feel ownership
- A forwarded email from them carries more weight than a cold email from you

The goal isn't to bypass gatekeepers. The goal is to turn gatekeepers into allies. Write emails that make them look smart for forwarding, and they'll do your prospecting work for you.
